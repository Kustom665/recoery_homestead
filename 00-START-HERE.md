# Start Here

The honest version. Read this before anything else in the repo.

## What you're building

A working-farm recovery community in Pennsylvania. Men in long-term recovery
live on the land, work it, and build it. The model is **Recovery Boys** — the
2018 documentary about Jacob's Ladder at Brookside Farm in Aurora, WV: a
six-month residential program built on daily farming, mindfulness, 12-step work,
chores, and structured community.

That model works. It is legal. There is real government money for it in
Pennsylvania right now. None of that is the hard part.

## The hard part

Three things kill projects like this, and all three are solvable if you know
about them before you spend money.

### 1. Campers are not legal permanent housing

In Pennsylvania, RVs and campers are **not permitted as permanent residences**
except inside a properly zoned RV park or mobile home park. This is the single
most common way a compound like yours gets shut down: someone reports it, the
township inspects, and residents get displaced.

You do not have to give up the campers. You have to use them correctly. See
`04-architecture/camper-phase.md`. The short version: buy land in a township
with no zoning ordinance, and use campers as construction-phase housing while
permanent cottages go up.

### 2. One big bunkhouse is a budget bomb

If you house a lot of unrelated residents in a single structure, the building
code can classify it as R-2 or even I-1 institutional occupancy. That triggers
fire sprinklers, commercial egress, and full ADA compliance. It can add six
figures you did not plan for.

A cluster of small detached cottages around a shared community building keeps
each unit in R-3 residential territory, which is vastly cheaper to permit. The
code strategy and the tiny-home vision point the same direction. Build the
cluster.

### 3. Licensure is not optional for you

DDAP recovery house licensure (Act 59 of 2017, implemented at 28 Pa. Code
Ch. 717, effective December 2021) is required if you accept state or federal
funding **or** referrals from state agencies.

Your entire funding plan is government money. So you will need it.

There is also a 2026 bill from Sen. Tracy Pennycuick that would require **all**
PA recovery residences to be licensed within three years. The direction of
travel is obvious. Build to the standard now rather than retrofitting later —
Ch. 717 carries physical-plant requirements, which means the architecture gets
designed backward from the licensing standard.

## One more risk, and it's the one nobody warns you about

**Resident labor has a federal wage-law edge.**

Residents building and farming the place is the therapeutic heart of the
Recovery Boys model. It is genuinely good for people. But work-based
rehabilitation programs have drawn federal Fair Labor Standards Act enforcement
over unpaid resident labor, and the analysis turns on details — who benefits,
whether the work is primarily therapeutic or primarily economic, whether the
program sells what residents produce.

Sweat equity is workable. The structure around it needs an attorney before the
first resident swings a hammer. This is a must-resolve, not a footnote.

## What I got wrong about Lancaster, and what to do instead

You floated Lancaster. The instinct isn't crazy — Lancaster is one of the more
permissive PA counties for long-term occupancy in rural and campground zones.
But the numbers kill it. Full breakdown in `02-land/lancaster-reality-check.md`.
Short version: ~$22,000/acre limestone farmland, the most aggressive farmland
preservation program in the country locking up ~125,000 acres, 99% of
southeastern PA municipalities carrying zoning ordinances, and limestone
geology that specifically disqualifies you from the sewage-planning exemption.

Land cost alone would eat the budget before you poured a footing.

**The unlock: 822 Pennsylvania municipalities have no zoning ordinance at all.**

In rural counties only 34% of municipalities have zoning. Northwest PA: 30%.
Central PA: 39%. Westmoreland County has no county zoning ordinance.
Susquehanna County has zoning in only 6 of its 40 municipalities.

In an unzoned township there is no variance to win, no hearing to survive, and
no neighbor petition to fight. The state building code and DEP sewage rules
still apply — those are not local — but the political battle simply does not
exist. This is the highest-leverage decision in the entire project.

## And if you do end up somewhere zoned

The Fair Housing Act is your weapon, not your fallback.

People in recovery from substance use disorder are a protected class under the
FHA, so long as they are not currently using illegal drugs. There is direct
Pennsylvania precedent: the United States sued the **Township of Cheltenham**
over a denied variance, and the township had to amend its ordinance to provide
reasonable accommodations. In **Janush v. Township of Upper Southampton**, a
1,000-foot group-home dispersion requirement was held to violate the FHAA.

A formal **reasonable accommodation request** is a different and stronger ask
than a variance, with a lower burden on you. Draft letter in
`03-zoning/fha-accommodation-request.md`. Send it through an attorney.

## The order of operations

Do not do these out of order. Each one gates the next.

1. **Form the 501(c)(3).** Almost every dollar in `06-money/funding-stack.md`
   requires it. This is the gating item. A fiscal sponsor is your bridge if you
   want to chase money before your own determination letter lands.
2. **Pick the county** using the four-axis score in `02-land/county-shortlist.md`.
3. **Find parcels in unzoned townships** in that county.
4. **Run the three kill tests** on any parcel before you spend a dollar —
   perc test, geology/nitrate check, easement check. See
   `02-land/parcel-due-diligence.md`.
5. **Get the money lined up** before you close, not after.
6. **Then** build, in the phases laid out in `04-architecture/site-plan.md`.

Week-by-week version in `07-action/90-day-plan.md`.

## What this repo is not

It is not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for the two professionals
you actually need:

- A **Pennsylvania land-use attorney**, especially for the zoning and FHA side.
- A **PA-licensed architect or engineer** for stamped drawings.

On this project both pay for themselves several times over. Budget for them in
Phase 0, not Phase 3.
