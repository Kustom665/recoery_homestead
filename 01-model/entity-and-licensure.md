# Entity and Licensure

## The entity

### Recommendation: 501(c)(3) nonprofit

Almost every dollar in `06-money/funding-stack.md` requires it — county opioid
settlement funds, USDA Community Facilities, PHARE, SAMHSA, CDBG, HUD. Without
it you are self-funding, and this project does not self-fund.

**Timeline:** roughly 3–6 months from incorporation to IRS determination letter,
sometimes faster with Form 1023-EZ if you qualify (there are revenue and asset
thresholds — your attorney will know whether you clear them).

**Steps:**
1. Incorporate as a PA nonprofit corporation (Department of State)
2. Adopt bylaws, seat an initial board
3. Obtain an EIN
4. File IRS Form 1023 or 1023-EZ
5. Register for PA charitable solicitation (Bureau of Charitable Organizations)

**Board composition matters more than people expect.** Funders read your board.
Aim for: someone with lived recovery experience, a clinician or licensed
counselor, someone with real estate or construction background, someone with
finance or accounting background, and someone connected to the county you are
building in. Five to seven people. Avoid a board of only friends and family —
funders notice.

### The bridge: fiscal sponsorship

If you want to chase money before your determination letter lands, a fiscal
sponsor — an existing 501(c)(3) — can accept grants on your behalf for a fee,
typically 5–10%. Your county Single County Authority or an established recovery
nonprofit can usually point you to one.

This is a legitimate, common arrangement. It gets you into a funding cycle you
would otherwise miss by a year.

### The hybrid worth discussing

A structure used often in this space: **a nonprofit that operates the program,
paired with an LLC that holds the land.** It separates operational liability
from the real property asset and keeps refinancing options open.

It also introduces self-dealing and private-benefit questions that the IRS cares
about. **Do not improvise this.** If you want it, have an attorney design it
before you form anything, not after.

## Licensure — DDAP

### You will need it

**Act 59 of 2017** gave the Pennsylvania Department of Drug and Alcohol Programs
(DDAP) authority to create a licensure program for recovery residences. It is
implemented at **28 Pa. Code Chapter 717**, effective **December 2021**.

Licensure is required for recovery houses that **receive state or federal
funding, or referrals from state agencies or state-funded facilities**.

Your entire funding plan is state and federal money. Your referral pipeline runs
through the county Single County Authority. **So licensure is mandatory for your
model.** There is no version of this that takes government money and skips it.

### The direction of travel

In April 2026, **Sen. Tracy Pennycuick** announced legislation to require **all**
established Pennsylvania recovery residences to obtain licensure within three
years — expanding well beyond today's conditional requirement.

Build to the standard now. Retrofitting a compound to meet physical-plant
requirements after it is built is dramatically more expensive than designing to
them.

### What Chapter 717 covers

The regulations contain provisions protecting residents in three areas:

- **Health and safety** — including physical plant requirements
- **Finances** — resident fee handling, refunds, accounting
- **Resident rights** — grievance procedures, discharge protections, privacy

**The physical-plant requirements are the ones that touch your architecture.**
Expect standards around occupancy limits per room, bathroom-to-resident ratios,
egress, fire safety and detection, food service, and sanitation.

### The critical sequencing point

**Read Chapter 717 in full before you finalize any floor plan.**

This is the single most common expensive mistake in recovery housing
development: design the buildings, then discover the licensing standard requires
something different. Bathroom ratios and occupancy limits in particular will
drive your cottage sizing and your bath house design.

Design backward from the standard. Not forward from a picture you liked.

### Where to start

- **[Apply to Become a Licensed Recovery House — DDAP](https://www.pa.gov/services/ddap/apply-to-become-a-licensed-recovery-house)**
- **[28 Pa. Code Part V — DDAP regulations](https://www.pacodeandbulletin.gov/Display/pacode?file=%2Fsecure%2Fpacode%2Fdata%2F028%2FpartVtoc.html&d=reduce)**
- **[DDAP Licensing — for professionals](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/ddap/for-professionals/licensing)**

Call DDAP licensing early — before you buy land. Ask what they see go wrong in
new applications. They will tell you, and it is free.

## Certification — PARR / NARR

Separate from licensure, and worth having.

The **National Alliance for Recovery Residences (NARR)** sets national standards
for recovery residences, with levels I through IV describing increasing
structure and support. Pennsylvania's NARR affiliate handles state
certification.

Your model — six-month residential, structured daily schedule, live-in house
manager, work component — maps to roughly **NARR Level II or III**.

**Why bother if licensure is already mandatory for you:**

- Many funders and referral sources ask for it specifically
- It gives you a ready-made operational framework rather than inventing policies
  from scratch
- It signals credibility to a county commissioner or grant reviewer who does not
  know you
- It is genuinely good practice, not just a credential

## Insurance

Do not leave this to the end. Lines you will need:

- General liability
- Property
- **Directors and officers (D&O)** — protects your board, and you will not
  recruit good board members without it
- Professional liability, if you provide any clinical or counseling services
- Workers compensation, once you have employees
- Vehicle, if you transport residents (you will)

Find a broker who has written recovery housing before. A generalist will either
misprice it or exclude the things that matter.

## The labor question — resolve it early

Flagged in `00-START-HERE.md`, repeated here because it belongs in the entity
conversation.

Residents building and farming the property is the therapeutic core of the
Recovery Boys model. It is genuinely good for people, and it is the heart of what
you described wanting to build.

But **work-based rehabilitation programs have drawn federal Fair Labor Standards
Act enforcement over unpaid resident labor.** The analysis turns on specifics:
who primarily benefits from the work, whether it is genuinely therapeutic or
functionally economic, and whether the program sells what residents produce.

That last one matters directly for you: **if the farm sells produce, the analysis
changes.** A farm that feeds residents is different from a farm that generates
revenue.

Bring this to your attorney at the entity-formation stage, before the first
resident picks up a hammer and before you plan any farm revenue. It is far
cheaper to structure correctly than to unwind.
