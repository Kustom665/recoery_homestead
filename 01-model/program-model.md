# Program Model

## The reference

**Jacob's Ladder at Brookside Farm**, Aurora, West Virginia — the program in the
2018 documentary *Recovery Boys*, directed by Elaine McMillion Sheldon. It
follows four men (Jeff, Rush, Adam, Ryan) rebuilding their lives after years of
opioid addiction.

**A six-month, long-term, mindfulness-based residential recovery program for
men, on a working farm.**

The reasoning behind the farm component: daily farming and gardening work as an
intentional intervention — physical, repetitive, outdoors, purposeful, with
visible results — aimed at re-wiring the neural patterns that addiction
reinforced. Work that produces something you can see at the end of the day is
doing something that a group room cannot.

### What a day looks like there

- Daily chores
- Morning and evening meditation
- Mindful farming
- Lecture and education
- Step work
- Structured free time
- 12-step meetings
- Community meetings

### The modalities

Mindfulness, meditation, and yoga; 12-step programming; music and arts; outdoor
recreation excursions; facilitated therapy; farming and sustainability.

## Why this model works, structurally

Four things are doing the work, and they are worth naming because they should
drive your design decisions:

**1. Length.** Six months, not 28 days. Long enough for the acute period to pass
and for genuine habit formation. Most relapse happens in the window that a
short program discharges people into.

**2. Purposeful physical work.** Not busywork, not job training as an
afterthought — work with visible output that the community depends on. Animals
that need feeding do not care how you feel today, and that is therapeutic.

**3. Community and accountability.** Everyone lives together, eats together,
works together. Isolation is a primary relapse driver. This structurally
prevents it.

**4. Structure.** A full day, every day, scheduled. Unstructured time is where
early recovery falls apart.

Your compound design should serve all four. That is why
`04-architecture/site-plan.md` puts the community building at the center, the
cottages facing inward around a common green, and the farm beyond the housing
so residents walk out to work and back in to rest.

## Your adaptation

| Element | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Population | Men |
| Program length | 6 months, plus a transition tier |
| Capacity at full build-out | 16–24 residents |
| Housing | Cottage cluster, 2 residents per cottage |
| Staffing | Live-in house manager, program director, farm lead |
| Core activity | Working farm + construction of the compound itself |
| Recovery framework | 12-step + mindfulness |
| Setting | 20–40 acres, rural Pennsylvania |

### The distinctive piece: residents build the place

This is the thing your version has that Jacob's Ladder does not, and it is
genuinely strong.

Residents arriving in Phase 0 and 1 live in campers and **build the cottages
that later residents will live in.** The therapeutic arc is unusually clean —
you arrive with nothing, you build something permanent, and it outlasts you.
Men who have spent years taking leave having made something.

It also happens to fund itself: sweat equity substitutes for construction labor
cost at the exact moment when capital is tightest.

**Two constraints on this, both real:**

- **The labor-law question.** See `01-model/entity-and-licensure.md`. Resolve it
  with an attorney before the first resident picks up a hammer.
- **Licensed trades stay licensed.** Electrical, plumbing, septic, well, and
  structural work require licensed professionals and inspections. Residents do
  site work, framing under supervision, finish carpentry, roofing, fencing,
  greenhouses, agricultural buildings, and the farm.

## The transition tier

Add this by Phase 4. A small number of more independent units for residents in
months five and six, moving toward leaving.

Why it matters: the cliff at discharge is where programs lose people. A
graduated step — more autonomy, more responsibility, still on the property,
still connected — measurably improves outcomes.

It is also a strong grant narrative. Funders have seen a hundred applications
for beds. Far fewer for a structured transition out of them.

## Referrals — the thing that determines whether you fill

**A recovery residence with no referral pipeline is an empty building with a
mortgage.** This is how these projects actually fail — not on permits, on
occupancy.

Your pipeline:

- **County Single County Authority (SCA)** — the local drug and alcohol
  commission. The single most important relationship. They control referrals.
- Detox and inpatient treatment facilities discharging people who need somewhere
  structured to go
- Drug courts and probation
- Hospital emergency departments and warm handoff programs
- Existing recovery community organizations

**Meet the SCA before you buy land.** Ask what they are short of. If the answer
is "we have nowhere to send men who need six months," you have validated the
model and earned a letter of support for every grant application you will ever
write.

## What to decide early

These shape the buildings and the license, so decide before you finalize plans:

- **Abstinence-based or MAT-friendly?** Whether you accept residents on
  medication-assisted treatment (buprenorphine, methadone, naltrexone) affects
  which funders will work with you, which referral sources will use you, and
  your medication storage requirements. Most public funders now strongly prefer
  MAT-friendly. Think hard before excluding it.
- **Fees.** Do residents pay? How much? What happens if they cannot? Chapter 717
  regulates resident finances — read it before setting a fee structure.
- **Family involvement.** Visits, family programming, and where that happens
  physically on the site.
- **Discharge policy.** What happens on relapse. This is the hardest policy in
  recovery housing and the one you least want to be improvising at 2 a.m.
- **Aftercare.** What connection continues after month six.
