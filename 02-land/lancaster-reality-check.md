# Lancaster Reality Check

You floated Lancaster. Here is the honest scoring, so you can decide rather
than take my word for it.

## Where the instinct was right

Lancaster genuinely is one of the more permissive Pennsylvania counties for
longer-term occupancy in rural and campground zones. If the only variable were
"will they let people live in a camper," Lancaster would rank well. That is a
real point in its favor and it is why the idea surfaced.

## Where it falls apart

### Land price

| Measure | Figure |
| --- | --- |
| Lancaster limestone farmland | **~$22,000/acre** |
| Southeastern PA farmland, general range | **$12,000–$25,000/acre** |
| Pennsylvania statewide median | **~$16,500/acre** |

Lancaster is some of the most expensive farmland in the United States, driven
by soil quality and sustained Amish demand. A 40-acre parcel at $22,000/acre is
**$880,000 for raw dirt** — before a well, before septic, before a single
structure.

The same money in a cheap county buys the land, the infrastructure, and the
first phase of buildings.

### Farmland preservation easements

Lancaster runs the most aggressive farmland preservation program in the nation,
approaching **125,000 preserved acres**. The county Agricultural Preserve Board
pays up to $4,000/acre for development rights; Lancaster Farmland Trust pays up
to $2,000/acre.

Once those rights are sold, the easement is **permanent and runs with the
land**. It prohibits exactly the non-farm residential development you are
proposing. A large share of the attractive, affordable-looking farm parcels you
will find listed in Lancaster are already preserved — and the listing may not
lead with that.

Any Lancaster parcel requires a deed and easement check before anything else.

### Nearly everything is zoned

**99% of southeastern Pennsylvania municipalities have zoning ordinances.**
Bucks, Montgomery, Berks, Lancaster — effectively fully zoned. Every parcel
comes with an ordinance, a hearing, and neighbors.

Compare: 822 PA municipalities statewide have no zoning at all, concentrated in
the northwest (only 30% zoned) and central (39%) regions.

### The limestone problem

This is the sharpest strike and the least obvious.

Lancaster's famous productive soil sits on **limestone — carbonate geology**.

Under Act 537, one of the requirements to qualify for an *exemption* from the
DEP sewage planning module is that the area proposed for on-lot systems **must
not be underlain by carbonate geology**. It also must not sit within a quarter
mile of water supplies with documented nitrate-nitrogen above 5 ppm — a real
concern in heavily farmed country.

So in Lancaster you would likely lose the exemption and be forced into a full
sewage planning module: more months, more consultants, more cost, at the exact
stage where you can least absorb it.

See `05-permits/sewage-act537.md` for the mechanics.

## Scored against the four axes

| Axis | Lancaster | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cheap land | **Fail** | ~$22k/acre; among the priciest farmland in the country |
| Unzoned townships | **Fail** | 99% of SE PA municipalities are zoned |
| Overdose burden / settlement money | Moderate | Real allocation, but not among the highest-burden counties |
| USDA rural eligibility | Partial | Some municipalities qualify, many exceed 20,000 population |
| Occupancy permissiveness | **Pass** | The one genuine advantage |
| Act 537 exemption available | **Fail** | Carbonate geology |

**One pass, one partial, four fails.**

## The verdict

Lancaster is a good place to *operate* a recovery community and a bad place to
*build* one from raw land on a tight budget. If someone donates you a
non-preserved Lancaster parcel with a passing perc test, take it. Do not go
shopping there.

Where to look instead: `02-land/county-shortlist.md`.
