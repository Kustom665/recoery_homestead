# The Camper Phase

You want campers. Here is exactly how they fit legally, and where they don't.

## The rule

In Pennsylvania, **RVs and campers are not permitted as permanent residences**
except when located in a properly zoned RV park or mobile home park. Tiny homes
on wheels (THOWs) are generally classified as RVs and inherit the same
restriction — placed temporarily is fine, permanent occupancy is not, and
utility hookups and long-term zoning are where it breaks down.

This is the single most common way an informal compound gets shut down.
Somebody complains, the township inspects, and residents — people in early
recovery, the most vulnerable moment of their lives — get displaced. That
outcome is worse than not building at all.

So do it right.

## Four legal paths

### 1. Unzoned township — the clean answer

If the township has **no zoning ordinance**, there is no zoning restriction on
occupancy. 822 PA municipalities qualify.

Still applies even here:
- **UCC building code** on permanent structures
- **Act 537 / DEP sewage rules** — this is the real constraint. Campers still
  generate sewage, and dumping it improperly is a DEP violation with teeth. You
  need a permitted septic system and a legal way to get waste into it.
- Well and water rules

**This is the recommended path.** It gets you flexibility without a fight.

### 2. Temporary use during construction — the honest bridge

Campers house the build crew while permanent cottages go up. Time-limited,
defensible, and easy to explain to any official who asks.

Many municipalities have an explicit temporary-use permit for exactly this — a
construction trailer or temporary dwelling during active building. Ask for it in
writing. Renewable, typically 6–12 months at a time.

The key word is **active**. This works while there is a real, visible,
permitted construction project underway. It stops working when the campers
become the permanent plan and the construction never progresses.

**Recommended: combine paths 1 and 2.** Unzoned township, campers as
construction-phase housing, permanent cottages as the end state.

### 3. Manufactured / mobile home park approval — permanent and legitimate

A properly approved mobile home park **is** a legal permanent path for
manufactured housing. More process — park approval, spacing standards, utility
and road standards, often a full land development submission — but it is real,
and it is the one route where the units can legally stay permanent.

Worth considering if you want to scale beyond 12 units, or if the campers are
genuinely central to the vision rather than a stepping stone.

Note the tradeoff: park standards impose density, spacing, and infrastructure
requirements that may cost more than just building cottages.

### 4. Campground / RV park zoning — mostly a trap

Some townships allow campgrounds and RV parks, and in some rural and campground
zones longer-term occupancy is tolerated. Lancaster County has historically been
among the more permissive here.

But campgrounds typically **cap stays** — commonly around 150 days or a single
season. That works for a seasonal cohort. It does not work for a year-round
six-month program with residents who need continuity.

Do not build the plan on this unless you have the specific ordinance in hand
and it says what you need it to say.

## Practical guidance for the camper phase

Whichever path, these apply:

**Sewage is the real constraint, not the campers.** Get the septic system
designed, permitted, and installed in Phase 0. Provide a legal, permitted
connection or a pump-out arrangement. Do not improvise this — DEP violations
are expensive and they generate exactly the kind of official attention you do
not want.

**Water.** Well drilled and tested in Phase 0. Potable water for the camper
phase comes from the same system that will serve the cottages.

**Winter is the hard part.** Pennsylvania winters are genuinely dangerous in an
uninsulated camper. Skirting, heated water lines, propane, and a **heated
common building** are not optional. This is the strongest practical argument for
building the bath house and community building in Phase 1, before any cottages:
it gives everyone a warm, safe place to be.

**Fire safety.** Campers burn fast. Spacing between units, working smoke and CO
detectors in every unit, extinguishers, and a clear evacuation plan. If you are
pursuing DDAP licensure — and you are — fire safety will be inspected.

**Keep it visibly temporary and visibly progressing.** Construction fencing,
materials on site, permits posted, visible progress month over month. An
official who sees an active build site treats you differently than one who sees
a permanent encampment. That difference is not cosmetic — it is the actual
legal distinction.

## The bottom line

Campers get you on the land fast and cheap, and they let residents start living
and working the property while they build the thing they will live in. That is
a genuinely good story and a genuinely good therapeutic arc.

Just don't let them become the permanent answer by default. Build the cottages.
