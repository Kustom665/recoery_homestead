# Site Plan

## The one decision that controls the budget

**Build a cluster of small detached cottages. Do not build one big bunkhouse.**

Here is why, and it is worth understanding rather than just accepting.

The building code classifies structures by **occupancy group**. A normal house
is **R-3**. A structure housing a larger number of unrelated residents can get
pushed into **R-2** (multi-family) or, if the code official reads supervision
and care into what you are doing, **I-1** (institutional, residential care).

What changes when you cross that line:

| Requirement | R-3 cottage | R-2 / I-1 building |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fire sprinklers | Often not required | **Required** |
| Egress | Residential stairs, standard windows | Commercial egress, rated corridors, two means of egress |
| Fire-rated construction | Minimal | Rated assemblies throughout |
| ADA / accessibility | Limited | **Full accessibility compliance** |
| Plan review | Routine | Architect/engineer stamp, full commercial review |

That difference can be six figures on a single building. It is the most
expensive avoidable mistake available to you.

**A cottage cluster keeps each dwelling in R-3 territory.** And it happens to be
exactly the tiny-home compound you described. The code strategy and the vision
point the same direction — that is rare, so take it.

Confirm the classification with your code official **in writing, early**. The
answer depends on how many residents per structure, whether staff live on site,
and how the program is described. See the language discipline section in
`03-zoning/zoning-strategy.md` — how you describe it genuinely affects how it
gets classified.

## Target program

Modeled on Jacob's Ladder at Brookside Farm: **six-month residential, men,
farm-based.**

| Element | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Residents at full build-out | 16–24 |
| Residents per cottage | 2 |
| Cottages | 8–12 |
| Program length | 6 months, with a transition tier |
| Land | 20–40 acres |
| Live-in staff | 1–2 (house manager residence on site) |

Start smaller than this. Phase 2 below gets you to 8–12 residents, which is
enough to prove the model, satisfy a funder, and not bankrupt you.

---

## Land requirements

**20–40 acres** is the sweet spot.

- Enough for meaningful agriculture (the therapeutic core), buffer from
  neighbors, and septic fields with replacement areas
- Small enough to stay affordable and manageable
- Under 20 acres, you lose the buffer and the farm becomes a garden
- Over 40, you are paying for land you cannot work yet

What the parcel needs:

| Requirement | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **Passing perc test, with replacement areas** | No perc, no project. Non-negotiable. |
| Road frontage on a township road | State road frontage means a PennDOT Highway Occupancy Permit — more time, more cost |
| Not carbonate geology | Preserves your Act 537 exemption |
| Not within ¼ mile of nitrate-contaminated supplies | Same |
| No conservation or ag preservation easement | Permanent, runs with the land, kills the project |
| Electric service reasonably close | Long service runs are expensive |
| Some tillable ground and some woods | Farm plus firewood, privacy, and a place to walk |
| Natural buffer from neighbors | Fewer complaints, less political risk |

## Phasing

Build in this order. Each phase produces something usable on its own, which
matters because funders release money in tranches and you need to show progress.

### Phase 0 — Control and prove the site

- Close on the land (after perc, geology, and easement checks pass)
- Written zoning determination in hand
- Well drilled, septic designed and permitted
- Electric service brought in
- Driveway and basic site work
- **Campers on site under temporary use** for the build crew — see
  `camper-phase.md`

This phase has almost nothing to show for it and consumes real money. Fund it
with the USDA loan and settlement money, not out of pocket.

### Phase 1 — The hub

**Build this before any housing.** It is what the code official and the licensing
inspector care about, and it is what makes a group of campers into a community.

- **Community building** — kitchen, dining, meeting room. This is where meals,
  meetings, and group work happen. The heart of the program.
- **Bath house** — showers, toilets, laundry. Centralized bathing keeps the
  cottages simple and cheap.
- Utilities distributed to the future cottage pads

### Phase 2 — Cottage cluster

- **4–6 cottages** to start, 2 residents each, on permanent foundations
- Arranged around a common green, facing inward — this is a real design
  decision, not decoration. Inward-facing clusters create passive supervision
  and a sense of shared space. Barracks rows do not.
- **House manager residence** — slightly larger, sited with sightlines across
  the common

### Phase 3 — The working farm

Agricultural buildings get UCC exemptions that dwellings do not, so this phase
is cheaper per square foot than anything before it.

- Barn / equipment building
- Greenhouses (season extension means year-round work, which matters for a
  year-round program)
- Livestock: chickens first, then goats or pigs
- Fenced growing fields

### Phase 4 — Scale and transition

- Cottages 7–12
- **Transition tier** — a few slightly more independent units for residents
  moving toward leaving. This dramatically improves outcomes and it is a strong
  grant narrative.
- Workshop / trades building, if the vocational component develops

## Layout principles

**Inward-facing common green.** Cottages face a shared open space with the
community building at one end. Everyone crosses the common to get anywhere,
which produces natural, constant, low-pressure contact. This is the single most
important design choice after the R-3 decision.

**Community building at the center of gravity.** Not at the edge, not at the
gate. Where people already walk past.

**One entrance.** Not for security theater — for accountability and for knowing
who is on the property.

**Farm beyond the housing, not between it.** Residents should walk out to work,
and walk back in to rest. That transition is psychologically real.

**Septic fields sited first.** They are the least flexible thing on the site.
Locate the primary and replacement fields, then place buildings around them.
People do this backward constantly and end up moving buildings on paper after
paying for the drawings.

**Room to grow.** Lay out all 12 cottage pads in Phase 1 even if you build 4.
Trenching utilities once is far cheaper than trenching three times.

## What needs a professional

You cannot do these yourself, and trying is a false economy:

- **Septic system design** — PA-licensed sewage enforcement officer and designer
- **Any structure beyond simple residential** — PA-licensed architect or
  engineer stamp. The community building will almost certainly need one.
- **Stormwater / erosion control plan** — County Conservation District
- **Site survey and subdivision plan** if you create lots

Budget these in Phase 0. See `06-money/budget-model.md`.

## Where the self-build actually happens

You said self-made, and that is the therapeutic core of the model. It is real,
but it lands in specific places:

**Good candidates for resident labor:** site work, framing under supervision,
finish carpentry, drywall, painting, roofing, decks and porches, fencing,
greenhouses, agricultural buildings, landscaping, the entire farm operation.

**Must be licensed and permitted:** electrical, plumbing, septic installation,
well drilling, structural engineering, anything requiring inspection sign-off.

**Read the labor-law warning in `00-START-HERE.md` before the first resident
picks up a hammer.** Work-based rehab programs have drawn federal Fair Labor
Standards Act enforcement over unpaid resident labor. Sweat equity is workable;
the structure around it needs an attorney first.
