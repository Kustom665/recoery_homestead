# Unit Specifications

## Design principle

**Every dwelling stays R-3.** Small, detached, simple. The moment a structure
starts housing enough unrelated residents to read as R-2 or I-1, the cost per
square foot roughly doubles. See `04-architecture/site-plan.md`.

**Design backward from 28 Pa. Code Ch. 717**, not forward from a picture.
Bathroom-to-resident ratios and occupancy limits in the DDAP licensing standard
will drive these numbers. Read the chapter before you finalize anything here.

## The resident cottage

| Spec | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Footprint | ~400 sq ft (20' × 20') |
| Occupancy | 2 residents |
| Foundation | Permanent — frost-protected shallow foundation or pier-and-beam |
| Bedrooms | 2 small private sleeping rooms |
| Bathroom | Half bath (toilet + sink). **Showers in the central bath house.** |
| Heat | Mini-split heat pump, or propane direct-vent |
| Ceiling | Vaulted, for volume without square footage |
| Porch | Covered, 6' deep, facing the common green |
| Estimated cost | $45,000 – $80,000 |

### Why half baths instead of full

Plumbing is the most expensive per square foot thing in a small building. Eight
full bathrooms cost dramatically more than eight half baths plus one well-built
bath house — and the bath house is a licensing asset, not a compromise.

It also serves the program. Centralized bathing means residents leave their
cottage and cross the common every morning. That is contact, and contact is the
intervention.

### Why private sleeping rooms

Two residents per cottage, each with their own small room, is a meaningful
upgrade over bunk-style housing:

- Better sleep, which matters enormously in early recovery
- Reduces conflict, which reduces discharges
- Reads as "home" rather than "facility" — which supports both the residential
  zoning classification and the FHA framing
- Likely aligns better with Ch. 717 occupancy standards

400 sq ft is enough for two ~90 sq ft sleeping rooms, a shared living space, and
a half bath. It is tight. It is also a private room, which many residents will
not have had in years.

## The bath house

| Spec | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Footprint | ~800 sq ft |
| Showers | 4–6 private stalls |
| Toilets | 4–6 |
| Sinks | 6–8 |
| Laundry | 2 washers, 2 dryers |
| Other | Utility sink, mop sink, storage, floor drains |
| Estimated cost | $96,000 – $200,000 |

High cost per square foot because of plumbing density. Build it once, build it
well, and oversize the water heater — running out of hot water at 6 a.m. with
sixteen men getting ready is a daily morale problem.

**Ch. 717 will specify fixture ratios.** Confirm before you build.

## The community building

The heart of the program and the most expensive single structure.

| Spec | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Footprint | ~1,800 sq ft |
| Kitchen | Commercial or near-commercial. **Check food service requirements** — feeding 16+ residents may trigger a commercial kitchen and Department of Agriculture food facility licensing. |
| Dining | Seats 24+ |
| Meeting room | 25+ people in a circle. Non-negotiable for 12-step work. |
| Offices | 2 small, one lockable for records and medication storage |
| Restrooms | 2, at least one accessible |
| Storage | Program supplies, pantry |
| Estimated cost | $180,000 – $360,000 |

**This will almost certainly need an architect or engineer stamp** and full
commercial plan review. Budget for it.

Two flags:

- **Medication storage.** If you accept residents on MAT, secure medication
  storage is required. Decide the MAT question early — see
  `01-model/program-model.md`.
- **The commercial kitchen question.** Cooking for 16+ residents daily may push
  you into commercial kitchen territory, which is a significant cost step.
  Confirm with the code official and the Department of Agriculture before
  designing the kitchen.

## House manager residence

| Spec | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Footprint | ~900 sq ft |
| Bedrooms | 2 |
| Bathroom | Full |
| Kitchen | Full |
| Siting | Sightlines across the common green |
| Estimated cost | $90,000 – $180,000 |

A live-in house manager is standard for a program at this level of structure,
and you will not recruit or retain one without decent housing. This is not a
place to economize.

## Agricultural buildings

**The cheap square footage on the project.** Agricultural buildings get UCC
exemptions that dwellings do not.

| Building | Estimated cost |
| --- | --- |
| Barn / equipment building | $40,000 – $120,000 |
| Greenhouses (2) | $15,000 – $50,000 |
| Fencing, livestock infrastructure | $10,000 – $35,000 |

Confirm the exemption applies to your specific structures and uses with the code
official — the exemption depends on genuine agricultural use, and a building
that is really a workshop or a dormitory does not qualify because you called it
a barn.

## Materials strategy

Ways to genuinely reduce cost without producing something that fails inspection
or falls apart:

- **Standardize.** One cottage design built eight times is far cheaper than
  eight variations — bulk materials, repeated crew learning, one plan review.
- **Simple rooflines.** Gable, not hipped. Complexity is where money goes.
- **Stock windows and doors.** Never custom sizes.
- **Metal roofing.** Higher upfront, 50-year life, and residents can install it.
- **Panelized or SIP options** for the cottages — worth pricing against stick
  framing. Faster dry-in, less waste, fewer weather delays.
- **Habitat for Humanity ReStores** for fixtures, appliances, and finishes.
- **Cultivate a lumber yard relationship.** A nonprofit building a recovery
  community is a story a local supplier may want to be part of. Ask for
  contractor pricing and in-kind donations. Many will say yes.
