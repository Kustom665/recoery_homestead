# Sewage — Act 537

**This is the thing that kills land deals.** Run it before you spend a dollar.

## Why it comes first

No septic means no dwellings, no occupancy, and no project. Not "harder" — over.
A parcel that will not perc is worth agricultural value and nothing more, no
matter how beautiful it is or how good the price looks.

Every offer you make gets an **inspection contingency for a passing perc test**.
Every one. No exceptions, no matter how motivated you are or how good the deal
seems.

## The two roads

Under Act 537, creating new lots or new land development normally requires a
**sewage facilities planning module** — a formal revision to the municipality's
official plan, reviewed by DEP. Months, consultants, and real money.

**But an exemption exists**, and qualifying for it is worth a lot to you.

## Qualifying for the exemption

For subdivisions served by on-lot sewage disposal systems, the exemption
requires **all** of the following:

1. **The municipal official plan must show the area as served by on-lot sewage
   disposal facilities**, confirmed by the appropriate municipal official.

2. **The area must not be underlain by carbonate geology** (as determined by
   DEP), **and** must not be located **within one-quarter mile of water supplies
   with documented nitrate-nitrogen concentrations exceeding 5 parts per
   million**.

3. **Soils testing and site evaluation must establish separate sites for both a
   permitted primary on-lot system and a replacement system on each lot** —
   including the residual tract, if any — confirmed by the **Sewage Enforcement
   Officer (SEO)** serving the municipality.

Requirement 3 is the one people underestimate. You need **two** viable areas per
lot: the primary field and a full replacement field. Plenty of parcels have one
good spot and no second one. That fails.

## Carbonate geology — read this before you look at Lancaster

Carbonate geology means limestone. Limestone bedrock is prone to sinkholes and
rapid groundwater transport, which is why DEP treats it as disqualifying for
the exemption.

**Lancaster County's famously productive soil sits on limestone.** So do parts
of Berks, Lebanon, York, and the Cumberland Valley.

Two things follow:

- In those areas you likely lose the exemption and face a full planning module
- Nitrate contamination is also a genuine concern in heavily farmed limestone
  country, which can trip the second half of requirement 2

This is a concrete, checkable reason to look at non-carbonate counties. Check
the DEP geologic mapping for any parcel before you get attached to it.

## How the testing actually works

The **SEO** for the municipality performs:

1. **Soil profile examination** — a backhoe pit, to read the soil horizons,
   depth to limiting zone, and depth to bedrock or water table. Often more
   informative than the perc test itself, and a bad profile ends the
   conversation early and cheaply.
2. **Percolation test** — timed water absorption in a set of holes, determining
   whether a standard septic system can be installed and how large the field
   must be.

Practical notes:

- **The SEO is a specific person for your municipality.** Get their name and
  number from the township early — they are one of the two or three most
  important people in this project.
- **Seasonal timing matters.** Some SEOs will not test in frozen ground or in
  the driest part of summer, because results are unrepresentative. Ask.
- Testing is inexpensive relative to the purchase. Do it before you close, not
  after.
- **Test where you actually want to build**, not just wherever the backhoe can
  reach easily.

## Sizing for your project

A 16–24 resident community generates substantially more flow than a single
family home. Consequences:

- You need a **larger system**, and possibly a **community system** rather than
  individual cottage systems
- Larger systems can trigger DEP permitting thresholds that individual systems
  do not
- **A community system may require a planning module regardless of the
  exemption**

**Get a septic designer involved before you finalize the site plan and resident
count.** Flow calculations should drive the phasing — not the other way around.
This is the most common sequencing mistake on projects like yours: people design
the community first and discover the sewage constraint after the drawings are
paid for.

## Alternate systems

If a standard system will not work, options exist — sand mounds, drip
irrigation, small-flow treatment facilities. They work. They cost
significantly more and add permitting time.

Know before you buy whether you are looking at a standard system or an alternate
one. The difference materially changes the budget.

## Checklist for any parcel

- [ ] Get the SEO's name and number from the township
- [ ] Confirm the municipal official plan shows the area as on-lot served
- [ ] Check DEP mapping for carbonate geology
- [ ] Check for water supplies within ¼ mile with nitrate-nitrogen over 5 ppm
- [ ] Soil profile examination in the intended building area
- [ ] Perc test — **primary and replacement areas**
- [ ] Preliminary flow calculation for your target resident count
- [ ] Determine: standard system, alternate system, or community system
- [ ] Determine: exemption available, or full planning module required
- [ ] Get a written estimate for design and installation
