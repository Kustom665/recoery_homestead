01 — The model

Proven, legal, and currently funded

The reference is Jacob's Ladder at Brookside Farm in Aurora, West Virginia — the six-month residential program in the 2018 documentary Recovery Boys. Daily farming, mindfulness, 12-step work, chores, structured community.

That model works. It is legal. There is real government money for it in Pennsylvania right now, and recovery housing is an explicitly eligible use of county opioid settlement funds. None of that is the hard part.

The hard part is that three specific things kill projects like this, and all three are solvable — but only if you know about them before you spend money. Everything downstream of here is about clearing them in the right order.

02 — Three tripwires

What actually shuts these places down

None of these are edge cases. Each one is a routine way a compound like this gets stopped, displaced, or priced out — and each has a known workaround.

  1. 01

    Campers are not legal permanent housing

    In Pennsylvania, RVs and campers are not permitted as permanent residences except inside a properly zoned RV or mobile home park. This is the single most common way a compound gets shut down: someone reports it, the township inspects, residents get displaced.

    Workaround — Buy in a township with no zoning ordinance and use campers as construction-phase housing while permanent cottages go up. The campers stay; their legal role changes.
  2. 02

    One big bunkhouse is a budget bomb

    House enough unrelated residents in a single structure and the building code can classify it R-2, or even I-1 institutional. That triggers fire sprinklers, commercial egress, and full ADA compliance — six figures nobody planned for.

    Workaround — A cluster of small detached cottages around a shared community building keeps each unit in R-3 residential territory. The code strategy and the tiny-home vision point the same direction.
  3. 03

    Licensure is not optional here

    DDAP recovery house licensure — Act 59 of 2017, implemented at 28 Pa. Code Ch. 717, effective December 2021 — is required if you accept state or federal funding or referrals from state agencies. The entire funding plan is government money, so it applies. A 2026 bill from Sen. Tracy Pennycuick would extend licensure to all PA recovery residences within three years.

    Consequence — Ch. 717 carries physical-plant requirements. The architecture has to be designed backward from the licensing standard, not retrofitted to it. Read it before any floor plan is final.
03 — The unlock

Build where there is no ordinance to fight

822 Pennsylvania municipalities have no zoning ordinance at all.

In rural counties only 34% of municipalities have zoning. Northwest PA runs 30%, central PA 39% — against 99% in the southeast. Nearly 35% of all land area across the twelve southwestern counties has no zoning of any kind.

In an unzoned township there is no variance to win, no hearing to survive, and no neighbor petition to fight. The state building code and DEP sewage rules still apply — those are not local — but the political battle simply does not exist. This is the highest-leverage decision in the project.

The county-backstop trap

"This township has no zoning ordinance" does not mean the land is unzoned. A county can adopt a countywide ordinance that fills in automatically wherever a municipality has none. Two of the counties people reach for first are exactly this case — Schuylkill adopted a countywide backstop effective 27 December 2010, and Fayette administers zoning for 31 of its 42 municipalities.

So the question is never "does this township have zoning?" It is "does this township have zoning, and does the county have a backstop ordinance?" Ask both, in writing, of the county planning office. That one question separates a by-right build from a nine-month hearing calendar.

And if you do land somewhere zoned

The Fair Housing Act is the weapon, not the fallback. People in recovery from substance use disorder are a protected class, provided they are not currently using illegal drugs. There is direct Pennsylvania precedent: the United States sued the Township of Cheltenham over a denied variance and the township had to amend its ordinance to provide reasonable accommodations. In Janush v. Township of Upper Southampton, a 1,000-foot group-home dispersion requirement was held to violate the FHAA.

A formal reasonable accommodation request is a different and stronger ask than a variance, with a lower burden on the applicant. Send it through an attorney.

04 — Where to build

The choice collapses to one question

Score every candidate on four axes, not one: cheap land, unzoned townships, overdose burden, and USDA rural eligibility. The third is the counterintuitive one — you want to build where the problem is worst. That is where the settlement money is, where the referrals are, and where a county commissioner returns your call.

Per-acre medians are computed across all parcel sizes, and small lots carry far higher per-acre prices than large tracts. A 30–40 acre wooded parcel should come in below the county median, not at it. Budget from the sold-price column, never the asking-price column. PA median runs ~$16,500/acre.

The honest way to decide

Susquehanna is the only county on the list where you can build by right — roughly 85% of it unzoned, no countywide backstop, and a planning commission that states in writing it "cannot, under its Ordinance, tell someone they cannot propose a specific type of development on a specific piece of land." Nothing else in the state combines all four axes. The price is a 2.5–3 hour drive from a Berks/Bucks base, and that cost is not small.

If you will realistically be on site several times a week for the first two years, distance beats zoning — take Schuylkill and budget for the land-use attorney. If this is a build-it-and-staff-it project where you are not the daily presence, take Susquehanna and build by right.

What you cannot do is split the difference: buying in a Schuylkill township with no municipal ordinance does not make you unzoned. The county ordinance covers you.

05 — Resident labor

The risk nobody warns you about

Must resolve before construction

Sweat equity has a federal wage-law edge

Residents building and farming the place is the therapeutic heart of the Recovery Boys model. It is genuinely good for people. But work-based rehabilitation programs have drawn federal Fair Labor Standards Act enforcement over unpaid resident labor.

The analysis turns on details — who benefits, whether the work is primarily therapeutic or primarily economic, and whether the program sells what residents produce. That last one matters here: the moment the farm sells produce, the calculus shifts.

Sweat equity is workable. The structure around it needs an attorney before the first resident swings a hammer. This is a must-resolve, not a footnote.

06 — The money

A stack that starts with one gating item

Almost every dollar requires a 501(c)(3). That is the first move, not the fifth. If you want to chase money before your own determination letter lands, a fiscal sponsor — an existing 501(c)(3) that accepts grants on your behalf for 5–10% — is the bridge.

The USDA grant is small — the loan is the point. Forty-year money with no fees, no prepayment penalty, and no prevailing wage requirement beats anything a bank will offer, and it is designed for exactly this. PHARE has funded ~400 initiatives across all 67 counties; confirm the current deadline directly with PHFA rather than relying on last year's date.

The relationship that decides whether beds fill

Every PA county has a Single County Authority — the local drug and alcohol commission. It is less a funding source than the entity that controls referrals, and a recovery residence with no referral pipeline is an empty building with a mortgage.

Meet them before you buy land. Ask what they are short of. If the answer is "we have nowhere to send men who need six months," you have just validated the entire model — and earned a letter of support for every grant application you will ever write.

That number compares very favorably to inpatient treatment or incarceration. Pull your county's real figures for the contrast and put them side by side in the narrative.

07 — Order of operations

Each step gates the next

These are genuinely sequential. Doing them out of order is how people end up owning land they cannot build on, or a permit they cannot fund.

  1. Form the 501(c)(3)

    The gating item — almost every funding source requires it. A fiscal sponsor bridges the gap while your determination letter is pending.

  2. Pick the county

    Score on all four axes. Confirm the county-backstop question in writing before going further.

  3. Find parcels in unzoned townships

    Within the chosen county, and only after the backstop question is answered.

  4. Run the three kill tests

    Perc test, geology and nitrate check, easement check. Any one fails, walk away. Touring parcels before this is how people fall in love with land they legally cannot build on.

  5. Line up the money before closing

    A signed agreement of sale with a long due-diligence period locks the parcel while the capital stack assembles.

  6. Then build, in phases

    Campers as construction-phase housing, cottages as the end state, designed backward from Ch. 717.
